The honest summary

A calibrated forecast, exact arithmetic, and a review pathway — not a treatment engine.

HemoTwin mirrors a dialysis session, projects the next hour of systolic blood pressure, and structures what the unit should review. It does not decide treatment. The what-if UF effect was tested and not found, so it stays clearly labelled as an illustration.

What we can claim
  • A calibrated pre-anchor forecast of the 60-minute cuff-sampled nadir, with a 90% band.
  • Exact volumetric fluid arithmetic, including what a scenario leaves behind for the next session.
  • A deterministic, versioned review pathway and a local, auditable session record.
  • Honest negative results: the what-if twin and the oracle comparison are reported as failures.
What we refuse to claim
  • That reducing UF early reliably raises SBP, or that any option is minimum-effective.
  • Validated alert utility, diagnosis, prescription, or autonomous machine action.
  • A treatment effect from the mechanism animation, or a per-minute confidence band.
  • External, prospective, symptomatic-IDH, or Indian-unit validation.

The one-sentence version

The forecast is useful as a projection, the arithmetic is trustworthy as arithmetic, and the review pathway is trustworthy as process. The treatment effect is not established — so the system shows the trade-off instead of pretending to know it.

The oracle trap, stated once

The 9.60 mmHg display forecast consumes 13 observed future UF features. It looks better because it already knows what the machine will do. It is a display-only card, not the serving model, and never a like-for-like comparison.

The sentence that must travel with the score

A low band is not safe, and a high band is not a diagnosis. In the curated five-chair demo, 1 high chair and 0 low chairs crashed — that is a story chosen to show the range, not an accuracy estimate.

Why A2 and not the better-looking model

The anchor-time models score higher (B: AUROC 0.8788). They also read BP and UF from roughly 90 minutes into the session, which makes them partly circular as a pre-session card. We accept the lower A2 score so the number is available before the first cuff.

What this changes for a unit

At a 25% referral burden (two of eight chairs), the board points attention at roughly 1.2 true crashes for every two chairs reviewed — while missing about 40% of crashes. That is why it is a review-attention surface, not a triage rule, and why the board order stays fixed by chair number. The value is in starting the conversation earlier, not in certifying anyone as safe.

Feature honesty: the planned UF goal encodes clinician behaviour, not physiology, and 391 first sessions without history are imputed from stored medians and flagged. A2 is observational — it can say “sessions that looked like this often crashed,” never “lowering this goal would prevent a crash.” Sources: CRASHRISK [3]; crashrisk pre-registration; evidence/chair_risk.json. Recall@25 and PPV@25 use the pre-registered top-25% referral set.

How to read a forest plot

The dot is the estimated effect; the horizontal line is its 95% confidence interval. If the interval crosses zero, the data cannot distinguish the effect from no effect. G7’s interval runs from −2.63 to +3.06 mmHg, so “no detectable effect” is the honest reading — not proof that UF never matters.

Sham control +2.91 mmHg passed its own pre-trend but failed as a placebo check; P6a +0.43 mmHg means the out-of-sample trajectory error got slightly worse, not better.

The other rows in the tab

Temporal-order diagnostic: of 397 actual-vs-hold rows, 234 UF cuts happened at the anchor with no preceding observed drop. That weakens the earlier “staff reacted to a fall” explanation, but it does not create a positive effect.

Protocol hash, FHIR bundle, audit chain: engineering evidence that the pathway and record are reproducible and exportable. They are not clinical evidence, and the hash chain is a local demo object, not an immutable clinical record.

The one line to take away

“5 of 6 pass” is an engineering scoreboard. The clinically decisive result is that the pre-registered UF effect test failed. A product that hides that failure would be less trustworthy than one that prints it.

The staffing gap behind the product

The person at the chair often has the least support and the most immediate decisions.

Haemodialysis is a high-frequency, high-consequence workflow: roughly three sessions per patient per week, with a doctor usually covering many chairs and sometimes several units. The gap is not another risk score. It is time, shared language, and explicit trade-offs.

The scenario the system is built for

In many district hospitals under the national dialysis programme, the chair is run by nurses or technicians with limited formal nephrology training; the nephrologist is remote and covers a large population. The Uttar Pradesh review is explicit: most dialysis at district hospitals is performed by nurses or technicians with limited working knowledge, and technician-course capacity is scarce. [7] This is not a criticism of the staff — it is a description of the support they are given.

A technician who learned from experience can recognise “this patient looks off,” but has little time to work through the fluid arithmetic, no shared vocabulary for a remote doctor, and no structured list of what to check before calling. The result is predictable: calls happen late, handoffs are verbal, and the same trade-off is rediscovered at every chair.

Why a support layer can make a difference

Not because it predicts better than a person. Because it mirrors the session (what has actually happened), structures the review (what to check, in what order), and shows the arithmetic (what a choice leaves behind). That buys the technician time and gives the remote nephrologist a shared, compact picture before the call.

The scale we are talking about

HEMOBP records a hypotensive nadir (<90 mmHg) in 10.4% of sessions. The Spanish cohort records clinician-defined symptomatic hypotension in 25.5% — different labels, never combined. Applying the 10.4% rate to an estimated ~34 million Indian sessions per year gives roughly 3.5 million hypotensive episodes a year. That is arithmetic on estimates, not a measured burden. [2][3]

And the staff already act

In HEMOBP, staff cut UF by more than 20% mid-session in 87.2% of sessions; the median rate falls from 0.58 to 0.10 L/h. The intervention is not missing — what is missing is a shared record of why, when, and what it cost. That is exactly the layer HemoTwin tries to add. [2]

Sources: ISN-GKHA 2023 / Lancet Global Health 2024 [4]; ISN Atlas income-group figures [5]; PMNDP official dashboard [6]; Indian J Nephrol, Uttar Pradesh services review [7]; HemoTwin FACTS [2]. The India density figure is a planning figure, not a census.

Attention is the scarce resource

Why a model with AUROC 0.88 still lost its alert authority.

An alarm budget is the number of false or non-useful alerts per session a unit is willing to absorb before staff start ignoring the system. A model can rank well and still fail at a fixed budget — because ranking is not the same as an operating point.

What it means on a ward

At eight chairs, 0.25 alerts per session is about two extra alerts per treatment round; an 18.2% false-alarm session rate is about 1.5 false alarms per round. That is the real cost: not the model’s error, but the nurse’s attention.

The event model is therefore shadow-only: it never pages, ranks, or gates the review pathway. The working alert is a measured rule (SBP below ~99 with a non-resetting 60-minute refractory), and even that is a retrospective replay result, not a prospective safety claim.

The trade-off in one sentence

Lower the threshold and you catch more crashes but drown the unit in alarms; raise it and you miss the ones that matter. The product exposes the threshold instead of hiding it.

The Chair board has a budget too: 25% referral

The pre-registered referral rule is “review the top 25% by risk” — at eight chairs, two chairs. On fold 2 that captures 60.15% of crashes and 59.14% of referred chairs crash. It misses about 40% of crashes and mislabels about 41% of referrals. That is why the board is fixed-order, display-only, and shifts attention rather than replacing it.

06 · THE FLUID MATHHEMOTWIN · SYSTEM BRIEF
Exact arithmetic, not a physiological prediction

Where the fluid goes when a session ends early.

The arithmetic answers a narrow, honest question: if this much UF is left unfinished, and this much extra fluid is given, how much is carried into the next session — and what does that do to the prescribed intensity? It does not predict SBP.

The five formulas

# goal, removed and carry are litres (converted to mL)
remaining = max(goal − removed, 0)
left = q_left × remaining
carry = left + extra_fluid
next_volume = next_prescription + carry

# reference flag, not a prescription limit
intensity = next_volumemL / (weightkg × durationh)
flag = intensity > 13 mL/kg/h

q_left is the fraction of the remaining prescription the scenario does not deliver (0 = finish, 1 = stop). extra_fluid is an added bolus or intake assumed to stay in the patient until session end. The 13 mL/kg/h line is the KDOQI reference ceiling used as a flag — not an order.

Worked example

InputValue
UF goal3.00 L
Already removed1.20 L
q_left (unfinished fraction)0.50
Extra fluid (bolus)0.25 L
Next prescription · duration · weight3.00 L · 4 h · 70 kg
remaining = 3.00 − 1.20 = 1.80 L
left = 0.50 × 1.80 = 0.90 L
carry = 0.90 + 0.25 = 1.15 L
next_volume = 3.00 + 1.15 = 4.15 L
intensity = 4150 / (70 × 4) = 14.8 mL/kg/h → over 13
Reading the worked example

Half-finishing the UF and giving a 250 mL bolus leaves 1.15 L to carry forward. The next session’s prescription is unchanged at 3.00 L, so its true starting load is 4.15 L — about 14.8 mL/kg/h, above the 13 reference. The arithmetic makes that cost visible; it does not say whether accepting it is clinically right.

The projection model (labelled illustration)

Case lab also draws a dashed mechanism sketch. It is an ODE with two fitted patient parameters and a literature-anchored immediate-refill term; the calibrated gain is 0, so it does not move the solid forecast. It is shown to make the physiology legible, not to claim an effect.

dD/dt = (1 − φ) · UF/60 − k · D # plasma-deficit D, UF in L/h
φ = 0.30 immediate refill fraction (Wang 2023 range 30–50%)
k ≈ 0.245 /h population refill rate (session IQR 0.10–1.31)
β ≈ 16.3 mmHg/L population SBP sensitivity to deficit
What each symbol means

φ: share of each litre removed that is replaced immediately by refill. k: how fast the remaining deficit refills. β: how many mmHg the pressure falls per litre of deficit. Gain: how strongly the simulation lets a UF change move SBP; it calibrated to 0.

Why it is not a treatment claim

The what-if test (G7) found +0.13 mmHg per 0.25 L/h, CI [−2.63, +3.06] — no detectable effect. At gain 0, actual and hold projections are identical (G3). The animation is honest only while it stays labelled as a mechanism illustration.

Assumptions shipped with the arithmetic: additional fluid stays in the patient; carry-forward is added only in the displayed scenario; 13 mL/kg/h is a reference flag. Sources: src/session_twin/fluid.py; KDOQI 2015 [10]; Wang 2023 / Mitsides 2019 [9]; G7 report [12].

07 · WHY INTERVENTIONS BREAK THE FORECASTHEMOTWIN · SYSTEM BRIEF
The hardest problem in the data

The nurse changes UF. The future changes too.

A forecast learns from observed sessions. In those sessions, staff already respond to falling pressure: they taper, pause, or give fluid. That makes the treatment and the outcome entangled — and it is why a model trained on the record cannot answer “what would have happened if we had acted differently?”

Conceptual timeline — illustration, not data

Why the simple version fails

1. Confounding by indication. Staff taper when the patient is already deteriorating. In the record, “UF was reduced” often precedes a crash — not because the taper caused it, but because it was a response to the fall. A model that learns that association learns the opposite of the causal lever.

2. The action is not known at prediction time. At the anchor, the future taper has not happened. A feature like “UF changed by −0.3 L/h at minute 95” cannot be used by a forecast made at minute 60 without leaking the future.

3. The policy changes when you deploy. If the console changes what staff do, the next data distribution changes too. A model trained on yesterday’s practice is calibrated to yesterday’s decisions.

4. The measurement is too sparse. Cuffs arrive roughly every 30 minutes. The immediate response to a UF change can wash out between readings, and the labels inherit that cadence.

5. The effect may be genuinely small. The G7 experiment estimated +0.13 mmHg per 0.25 L/h with a CI from −2.63 to +3.06 — indistinguishable from zero. That does not prove UF never matters; it does mean the data cannot support a confident positive claim at this scale.

The practical consequence

The what-if sketch is off by default. The console shows arithmetic (what a choice leaves behind) and a projection under the observed trajectory — not a promise about what an intervention will do.

The difference in one line

What the model learns: P(crash | a taper happened). What a decision needs: P(crash | we choose a taper). Those two are equal only if tapers were assigned at random — and they were not: staff tapered because the patient was already falling. That is confounding by indication, and no extra feature removes it without a change in design.

Supporting facts: staff cut UF >20% mid-session in 87.2% of sessions; median 0.58 → 0.10 L/h; 81.7% end below 80% of peak UF [2]. G7: 581 matched events, 158 patients, MDE 2.33 mmHg; sham +2.91; P6a +0.43 [3][12].

07 · WHY INTERVENTIONS BREAK THE FORECASTHEMOTWIN · SYSTEM BRIEF
So can we just add “UF changed ±X” as a feature?

Yes for nowcasting. No for causality.

Action history is useful — it is one of the strongest signals that a clinician has seen something the cuffs have not yet shown. But it answers a different question from “what will this action do?” The distinction decides what you are allowed to ship.

The route we would take next

1 · Log the action, not just the state. Every UF change gets a timestamp, a rate delta, a duration and an optional reason. This is the dataset the current record lacks — and it is a prerequisite for every option above.

2 · Separate the two products. Keep the state mirror and the observational forecast honest; build any effect estimator as a separate, clearly labelled research surface with its own validation.

3 · Start with nowcasting. Test whether past-action features improve next-cuff prediction under a patient-disjoint split. If they do, ship them as a prediction feature — never as a treatment recommendation.

4 · Then identify an effect. Use target-trial emulation with time-varying confounders, or a protocolised/randomised step-down with a pre-registered SBP endpoint. G7 shows the effect is small enough that the design, not the model, is the limiting factor (MDE 2.33 mmHg).

5 · Increase the measurement density. Continuous or 5-minute BP, plus symptom and action labels, would let the data see a response that 30-minute cuffs cannot resolve.

The discipline

Never let a prediction model masquerade as a treatment effect. The current console enforces that separation in its copy, its gates, and its default view.

What “adding the feature” would actually do

If you add “UF changed” to a model trained on historical sessions, the model will likely learn the clinician’s policy: tapers happen when pressure falls, so the feature is a proxy for deterioration. The model may predict the next cuff better and still be useless as a guide to action. That is not a bug in the feature; it is the difference between prediction and intervention. The only way to close that gap is a design that makes the action exogenous — a protocol, a randomised step, or a defensible natural experiment — plus a pre-registered effect estimate.

Directions are research plans, not current capabilities. See EVIDENCE_STATUS “Next evidence required” [3].

08 · INTO THE WORKFLOWHEMOTWIN · SYSTEM BRIEF
What the nurse actually sees

A five-step loop that fits the chair, not a new workflow.

The console is designed around the moments that already exist: before the first cuff, at each cuff, when something looks wrong, and at handoff. It adds structure to those moments without taking the decision away from the team.

01 · PRE-SESSION
Look at the board
Fixed-order chairs with an A2 risk band. No BP, no current UF. Attention cue only.
02 · START
Enter the session
Starting BP, UF goal, dry weight, symptoms. The mirror begins; missing data is flagged.
03 · EACH CUFF
Read the projection
Nadir forecast, 90% band, protocol state, stale-cuff guard. The forecast never sets the state.
04 · IF REVIEW
Work the pathway
Assess, repeat BP, review UF, check fluid. Role-scoped steps, deterministic order.
05 · HANDOFF
Export the record
FHIR bundle, bilingual card, frozen evidence, local audit chain. Replay for teaching.

What it helps with. Time: the projection and the checklist shorten the “what do I do now?” pause. Shared language: the same five views travel with the patient from the chair to the phone call. Memory: the replay and audit chain preserve what was seen and done.

What it does not do. It does not alarm, prescribe, allocate chairs, or act on the machine. The protocol state is driven by measured rules (SBP, symptoms, drop from baseline), not by the forecast. Missing required data produces INSUFFICIENT_DATA, not a guess.

Integration path

Today: manual entry or CSV on a local machine, with no machine connection and no network call. Next: a read-only machine-log adapter and a local deployment package. Later: identity, role enforcement, and a validated interface — all require a pilot and ethics approval first.

Who does what

Technician / dialysis nurse

Enters cuffs and UF; reads the band and the state; works the pathway; escalates. The primary user at the chair.

Remote nephrologist

Receives the handoff; sees the same numbers; decides. The console is a shared picture, not a second opinion engine.

Unit lead / clinical engineer

Owns the protocol version, the local calibration, the audit review, and the override log for any pilot.

The current prototype has no authentication, no role enforcement, no machine integration, and no external notification. The audit chain is a local demo object. These are prototype limits, not hidden features.

What would make this real

Earn the right to predict, then earn the right to advise.

The next phase is not a bigger model. It is better evidence, denser measurement, and a clear separation between a mirror, a forecast, and a treatment effect.

Now · 0–6 months
  • Local Indian cohort + label harmonisation (SBP<90 vs symptomatic).
  • Log every UF change with time, delta, duration, reason.
  • Prospective silent-mode run: does the workflow fit the chair?
  • Recalibrate on the local unit; publish subgroup metrics.
Next · 6–18 months
  • Action-aware nowcasting, evaluated as prediction only.
  • Pre-registered causal pilot (target trial or protocolised step-down).
  • Continuous / 5-minute BP feasibility and symptom labels.
Later · 18 months+
  • Wearable fusion — only after incremental-value studies.
  • Read-only machine integration and local deployment package.
  • Regulatory strategy (CDSCO SaMD, DPDP Act 2023).
  • Prospective outcome evaluation, not a model demo.

Could wearables add signal?

Yes — as research features, not as a cuff replacement. Dialysis is a 3–5 hour window with repeated sitting still, which is unusually friendly to optical and motion sensing. The constraint is not the sensor; it is validation, missingness, and workflow.

What a credible wearable study would require

Clock synchronisation to the cuff and machine; a prespecified incremental-value metric (does it improve MAE or recall at the same alarm budget?); patient-disjoint validation; handling of missing and artefactual windows; and a clear separation of monitoring from alerting.

What would not be credible

A wearable demo that correlates with BP in a convenience sample. The bar is incremental value over the measured cuff + UF state, with the same clinical claim boundary.

The product thesis, unchanged

The gap is time and a shared trade-off. A mirror, a structured review, and honest arithmetic buy the technician enough time to react and seek advice. Prediction is one feature of that — not the product.

The boundary, in full

We can claim: a calibrated pre-anchor nadir forecast; exact fluid arithmetic; a deterministic, versioned review pathway; honest negative results from the twin and oracle tests; a local FHIR export and hash-chained demo audit.

We refuse to claim: validated alert utility, clinical benefit, or a safety layer; a treatment effect from any UF option; prospective, external, symptomatic-IDH, or Indian-unit validation; authentication, role enforcement, immutable audit, or machine integration.
